01What the system actually is

A conventional engine instrument system watches each channel against a fixed redline. On a light aircraft at 8,000 ft that works. On a MALE UAV climbing to 30,000 ft on a twenty-hour sortie it fails in both directions at once.

The two failures of a fixed threshold

Climb high enough and a healthy engine starts tripping alarms. Manifold pressure falls as the turbocharger runs out of air, rated power derates from 200 HP to 110 HP, exhaust temperatures drift. Nothing is broken — the atmosphere changed. The operator learns to ignore the alarm.

Meanwhile a genuinely failing engine sits comfortably inside every redline. An injector clogging on cylinder 3 pushes EGT₃ up by 40 °C, well under the 880 °C continuous limit, so nothing trips — right up until it does.

The trap

A raw sensor reading carries two things at once: the state of the engine, and the state of the flight. You cannot threshold your way out of that, and a black-box model trained on raw telemetry inherits the same problem — it just hides it behind a confidence score.

What EngineTwin does instead

A Mean Value Engine Model (MVEM) runs in lockstep with the engine, fed the same altitude, ambient conditions and throttle. It predicts what a healthy engine would be doing right now. Diagnosis then happens on the difference, normalised per channel:

rᵢ = ( y_measured − y_physics ) / σ_channel

Fifteen channels — RPM, manifold pressure, fuel flow, oil pressure and temperature, four CHT, four EGT, vibration RMS and bus voltage — reduce to one vector of standard deviations from thermodynamic expectation.

Because the physics baseline has already absorbed altitude, throttle and ambient temperature, a healthy engine sits at r ≈ 0 everywhere in the envelope. The learning problem stops being “recognise this engine” and becomes “recognise a departure from physics” — a far smaller and far more transferable problem.

The one sentence to remember

Every model downstream sees residuals. None of them ever sees a raw sensor value. That single decision is what the whole system rests on.

02Starting it up

Requirements

Python 3.10 or 3.11, and a browser with WebGL. Nothing else.

First run

  1. Create an environment and install.
    python -m venv .venv .venv\Scripts\activate # Windows source .venv/bin/activate # macOS / Linux pip install -r requirements.txt
  2. Launch. python main.py, or double-click run_demo.bat on Windows. The launcher opens http://127.0.0.1:8000 automatically.
  3. Wait for warm-up. The twin runs forward 15 seconds before accepting clients, so the cylinder thermal states and the estimator have settled. Without it the first frame would read CRITICAL on a perfectly healthy engine.
  4. You land on the cover. Scroll down through it, or press End to jump to the Continue button.
No internet required

Tailwind, Chart.js, Three.js and the webfont are all vendored under dashboard/vendor/ and served from the twin’s own host. The station comes up on a closed network. Refresh those copies only if you need to, with python tools/fetch_vendor_assets.py.

If FastAPI is missing

main.py falls back to standalone_server.py, which serves the whole twin on the Python standard library alone. Telemetry then arrives over Server-Sent Events instead of a WebSocket; the dashboard switches transports by itself and nothing else changes. NumPy and PyTorch are still required for the physics and the networks.

05Reading the graphs

All three charts share one convention: solid lines are what the sensors measured; dashed violet is what the physics model expected. The distance between them is the residual, and the residual is the entire diagnosis. If solid and dashed sit on top of each other, the engine is doing what physics says it should — whatever the absolute numbers are.

Chart 1 — Exhaust & head temperature

SeriesStyleMeaning
EGT 1–4Four solid neutral traces, lightest to darkestExhaust gas temperature per cylinder. Told apart by lightness, never hue.
Model expectedDashed violetWhat the MVEM says EGT should be right now.

A trace turns amber above 880 °C and red above 950 °C, and the chart carries tinted bands at those levels. A single EGT climbing away from its three siblings is the signature of a mixture fault on that cylinder. All four rising together is usually the flight condition, not a fault — which is exactly what the dashed line will confirm.

Chart 2 — Oil pressure & manifold boost

The altitude test

Watch MAP during a climb. The solid line falls steeply — and the dashed line falls with it, so the residual stays flat and no alarm fires. That is the entire argument of the project, visible in one chart.

Chart 3 — Order-tracked vibration & power

SeriesAxisMeaning
Vibration 2X RMSLeftVibration at twice crankshaft order, g RMS. Amber at 2.5 g, red at 4.0 g.
PowerRight, dashed violetModel shaft power, HP.

Bearing wear shows as slow growth in the 2X order, not as a step. Watching this trace rise over a minute while power holds steady is the classic developing-mechanical-fault picture.

Root-cause attribution — horizontal bars

Not a time series. When a fault is named, this panel ranks the top five residual channels that drove the decision, with the percentage each contributed and how many sigma it sits from expectation. Bars turn amber or red as that channel's own residual leaves tolerance.

What this is, precisely

Gradient × input attribution, not Shapley values. It gives a defensible ranking of which channels moved the decision, not an additive decomposition with SHAP’s guarantees. It was chosen because it costs one backward pass and fits a 50 ms edge budget.

06The 3D engine model

The model is an instrument, not decoration. It is a procedural VRDE 2.2 L four-cylinder turbocharged aero-diesel, and everything it shows is driven by the live twin state.

How components are coloured

Each cylinder is shaded by its thermal deviation from the model, measured in sigma — not by absolute temperature. A cold-day 700 °C and a hot-day 760 °C both read as nominal; only a genuine departure changes colour.

DeviationStateAppearance
< 2.0 σNominalNeutral metal
2.0 – 4.0 σCautionAmber
≥ 4.0 σWarningRed

The oil sump, turbocharger and crankcase are coloured by their subsystem health score instead, going amber below 85 % and red below 50 %. When a fault is active, the implicated component is outlined and the outline breathes slowly so it reads as a live annunciation.

08Predicting faults — how a diagnosis is produced

Twenty times a second the service runs the following chain. Understanding the order matters, because each stage is allowed to stop the ones after it.

flight profile -> MVEM integrate -> apply faults -> sensors sample | +-> estimator predict/update -> residuals r = (y - y_hat)/sigma | +-> health indices (always) | +-> anomaly trigger --(below threshold)--> tick ends | +-> sensor validator --(probe fault)--> report and stop | +-> classifier -> severity | +-> attribution + RUL -> operator card

Stage by stage

  1. Flight profile advances. ISA atmosphere gives ambient pressure and temperature for the current altitude; the mission phase sets throttle. Phases run TAXI_WARMUP, CLIMB_TO_ALTITUDE, HIGH_ALT_LOITER, DESCENT, APPROACH_LANDING.
  2. MVEM integrates. Manifold filling, turbo boost with altitude derating, crankshaft torque balance, four cylinder thermal states, the oil circuit. This is ground truth.
  3. Faults are applied, if any are active, as parametric perturbations of the physics or the instrumentation.
  4. Sensors sample that truth through per-channel noise, first-order lag, quantisation and sample rate.
  5. The estimator fuses measurement and physics into a 12-state estimate. If the datalink drops, it coasts on physics instead of producing a spurious residual spike.
  6. Residuals are computed against a second MVEM that never saw the fault, and normalised per channel.
  7. Health indices map residuals to bounded 0–100 % scores. This happens every tick regardless of what follows.
  8. Anomaly trigger. An autoencoder trained only on healthy residuals reconstructs the vector. If the error clears mean + 3σ of the healthy calibration distribution, the rest of the stack wakes up. Otherwise the tick ends here — which is why the classifier only runs on about 1.2 % of frames.
  9. Sensor validator decides, before anything else, whether this is a bad probe or a bad engine.
  10. Classifier returns a calibrated fault class and a continuous severity.
  11. Attribution and RUL attach the evidence and the remaining-life interval.
Why the trigger comes before the classifier

The autoencoder is unsupervised — it flags anything unlike healthy flight, including failure modes absent from the taxonomy. Gating the classifier behind it also saves 52 % of inference CPU, because during nominal flight the expensive stage never runs.

Reading a diagnosis

FieldHow to read it
ConditionThe named fault class, or HEALTHY. Coloured by severity.
ConfidenceTemperature-scaled, so it means something. An uncalibrated network reports 99 % on marginal cases; this one does not.
SeverityContinuous 0–1 from a separate regression head, independent of class confidence.
Anomaly index0–1, derived from reconstruction error against the calibrated threshold.
RUL windowRemaining life in flight hours, as a range. See the caveat below.
Sensor fault flagTrue when the validator attributes the anomaly to instrumentation rather than the engine.
The RUL number is a trend interval, not a calibrated one

It is built from the spread of the degradation-trajectory fit, not from a distribution-free coverage guarantee. Do not describe it as a 95 % confidence interval. When health has already fallen below the 30 % condemnation threshold it reports zero, because reporting hours remaining on an engine that is already past its limit — while the banner says return to base — would leave an operator believing neither number.

Predicting rather than reacting

To watch prediction rather than detection, inject a slow fault and leave it running: bearing wear at 5× rate is the clearest. The 2X vibration trace climbs gradually, overall health drifts down, and the RUL window narrows step by step. The previous estimate stays on screen beneath the current one, so the revision itself becomes evidence that the trend is real.

09Manual flight sandbox

The sandbox lets you fly the aircraft yourself instead of following the scripted mission. It is how you demonstrate the central claim: that a healthy engine stays healthy across the whole envelope.

Controls

Mode: auto missiontoggle

Switches between the scripted profile and manual control. In manual, the three sliders command the aircraft.

Throttle0 – 100 %

Commanded throttle. Slews at 25 %/s.

Altitude0 – 35,000 ft

Commanded altitude. Slews at 3,000 ft/min. The ISA atmosphere is recomputed at the commanded altitude, so the turbocharger genuinely sees thinner air.

OAT−50 – +45 °C

Outside air temperature. Slews at 6 °C/s.

The sliders are setpoints, not teleports

Commanded values are slewed at rates the airframe can actually fly. An instantaneous altitude step is not a manoeuvre — it is a discontinuity, and because the sensors carry first-order lag while the model baseline does not, a step opens a residual gap the classifier correctly reads as a real turbo fault. Slewing keeps the sandbox inside the physics the twin was built for. Rates are per second of simulation time, so 5× and 10× speed the climb up with everything else.

The demonstration that matters

  1. Open Sandbox and switch to Mode: manual sandbox. The aircraft is picked up wherever the mission left it.
  2. Set the rate to 5x or 10x so the climb completes in about a minute of real time.
  3. Set throttle to 95 % and altitude to 0 ft. Let it settle.
  4. Drag altitude to 30,000 ft and watch.

10Recording, debrief and replay

What gets recorded

The flight data recorder starts with the server and records automatically — there is no button to arm it. Every broadcast frame is appended to disk in data/flight_logs/<sortie-id>/:

FileContents
meta.jsonSortie header: start time, duration, frame count, faults seen, minimum health, worst band
frames.jsonlOne serialised twin frame per line, in time order
Why JSONL

Frames are appended during flight with no rewrite, the log can be inspected with ordinary tools, and a sortie interrupted by a hard shutdown still reads back to its last complete line.

Lists recorded sorties, newest first, with duration and fault count. The one currently recording is marked.

▶ Replay

Streams the selected sortie back through the live telemetry path.

■ Live

Returns to live telemetry. The simulation kept running underneath, so you rejoin a mission in progress.

The post-flight report

Selecting a sortie produces a report answering what an operator asks after landing:

  • Duration, frame count and altitude range flown.
  • Health minimum and mean, and the worst band reached.
  • Time in each health band — how long the engine spent NOMINAL, ADVISORY, CAUTION, WARNING and CRITICAL.
  • Time in each mission phase.
  • Per-subsystem health minima across the whole sortie.
  • Trigger-to-diagnosis latency — the gap between the unsupervised trigger firing and the classifier committing to a name. This is measurable from the log without ground truth.
  • The diagnosis timeline — each event with its class, onset time, duration, and the residual channels that drove it.
The timeline is debounced

Per-frame classification flickers, and at 20 Hz a single ambiguous frame would otherwise open and close a 0.05 s “event”. A class change is only accepted once it has persisted for a second — the same rolling-vote discipline the live annunciator uses, applied to the log.

Replay behaves exactly like live

Replay is a property of the stream, not of the twin. Recorded frames are pushed down the same transport the live twin uses, so the whole console — telemetry strips, health matrix, 3D view, evidence drawer — replays without modification. The playhead is advanced by the simulation clock rather than by consumer reads, so replay runs at true speed no matter how many clients are attached.

11Demonstration scripts

Five minutes, unattended

Press ▶ Auto demo and let it run. Forty seconds, six phases, hands-free. Best when you need to talk over it.

Five minutes, driven

  1. Watch it loiter. High-altitude cruise. Every strip shows the measurement tracking the physics baseline. Point out that the absolute numbers look nothing like sea level, and nothing complains.
  2. Break the oil system. Hit Oil pressure loss. Under three seconds later the trigger fires, the classifier names it, the sump goes red on the model, and the directive reads return to base.
  3. Break a sensor instead. Clear, then hit Probe defect (EGT3). EGT₃ falls toward ambient — the case a redline system aborts a healthy aircraft over. Health barely moves; the verdict is sensor probe defect, engine healthy.
  4. Ask it why. Hit Bearing 2X vib and read the attribution panel, or click the crankcase in the model to open the inspector on the vibration channel.
  5. Fly the envelope. Section 09 — climb to 30,000 ft at 5× and watch power derate while health holds.
  6. Land and debrief. Open Debrief, pick the sortie, read the report, then replay it.
If you only have ninety seconds

Do step 3 and step 5. The probe defect shows what nothing else on the market does, and the climb shows why the whole architecture exists.

Injecting a fault

curl -X POST http://127.0.0.1:8000/api/fault/inject \ -H "Content-Type: application/json" \ -d '{"fault_type": "TURBO_BOOST_DEFICIENCY", "severity": 0.7, "ramp_duration_s": 12.0}'

Reading the stream

import asyncio, json, websockets async def listen(): async with websockets.connect("ws://127.0.0.1:8000/ws/telemetry") as ws: while True: s = json.loads(await ws.recv()) ai = s["ai_prognostics"] print(f'{s["timestamp_s"]:7.1f}s {s["altitude_ft"]:6.0f}ft ' f'health {s["health"]["overall_health"]:5.1f}% ' f'{ai["fault_class"]}') asyncio.run(listen())

Each frame carries the flight state, every raw sensor channel, the model expectation for each, the filtered estimate, the residual vector, seven health scores and the AI block. The full schema is digital_twin/twin_state.py.

Simulation advances on a background task, so /api/state is populated with no browser attached and a second dashboard tab observes the same sortie rather than stepping it a second time.

13Verifying the claims

Every number in this handbook comes out of a script in the repository.

python run_all_tests.py # all nine suites, ~2.5 min python test_simulation.py # MVEM, sensors, flight profiles python test_ai_layer.py # estimator, autoencoder, classifier, RUL python test_fault_pipeline_qa.py # end-to-end detection per fault mode python validation/benchmark.py # the comparison table in section 01 python validation/ablation.py # which choice is load-bearing python validation/leakage_audit.py # train/test separation integrity python validation/stress_testing.py # 5x noise, 30 s blackout, 35,000 ft python tools/edge_benchmark_profiler.py # the latency budget

Every suite exits non-zero on failure, so a green run means all nine genuinely passed. The ablation is the interesting one if you are sceptical: it retrains four networks on your machine and prints numbers it computed, rather than numbers anyone recorded.

Rebuilding data and models

python data/generate_dataset.py # 78 independent sorties python train_and_export_models.py # retrain, recalibrate threshold
Only one script rewrites the committed datasets

data/generate_dataset.py is it. The test suite generates into a temporary directory, because overwriting the CSVs mid-run would leave the shipped checkpoints scored against data they were never trained on — which is exactly the failure that once made the benchmark report the system losing to its own baseline.

15Known limitations

Stated here rather than left to be discovered. A reviewer who finds an unstated limitation discounts everything else in the submission.

1 — Everything is simulated

The engine, the instrumentation and the faults are models. No hardware-in-the-loop validation has been performed, and nothing here is certified for airworthiness or operational flight.

2 — The estimator is not an EKF

It is a linear Kalman filter with a complementary prediction step toward the physics model, with F = I and H = I. Because F is identity, the covariance is a tuned noise-rejection parameter rather than a calibrated uncertainty. Anything needing meaningful state covariance would require a true EKF or UKF.

3 — The attribution is not SHAP

It is gradient × input: a first-order local ranking without Shapley’s local-accuracy or consistency guarantees. Chosen for the edge budget. shap.DeepExplainer runs offline against the same network where exact values are wanted.

4 — The RUL interval is a trend interval

Constructed from the spread of the degradation fit, not from a distribution-free coverage guarantee. Conformal prediction is the honest upgrade.

5 — The sensor validator covers two archetypes

Thermocouple open circuit and oil transducer dropout. It isolates 93.3 % of held-out probe-defect frames, not 100 %. The ablation further shows the trained classifier already separates probe defects on its own, so the validator currently buys no measurable accuracy — its value is that it is a physics rule rather than a learned boundary, and so holds for probe failures absent from the training set.

6 — Per-frame inference degrades under extreme noise

At five times instrumentation sigma the classifier names a false mechanical fault on about one frame in six. The annunciator votes over a rolling window so this does not reach the operator, but it is a real weakness of per-frame inference on out-of-distribution noise.

7 — The MVEM fit figure is near-circular

The model interpolates the same altitude derating table it is then scored against, so the 0.00 HP RMSE is a consistency check on the interpolation, not independent validation. Manifold pressure, which is not fitted to the table, tracks less exactly.

8 — The taxonomy is nine modes plus healthy

Real engines fail in compound and cascading ways this taxonomy does not name, though the unsupervised trigger will still flag them as anomalous — it just will not name them.

Where this goes next

Bench validation against a test-cell engine, recalibration on real data, an edge port to flight-representative compute, then secure telemetry and a requirements trace. The four stages and what each requires are chapter 21 of the cover journey.
